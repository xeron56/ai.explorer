After you identify trend direction, the next skill is reading pullbacks.

In a downtrend, pullbacks are the small upward bounces that appear between stronger selling waves. In an uptrend, pullbacks are the small downward dips that appear between stronger buying waves. Both are temporary moves against the dominant direction — not trend changes.

## Impulse versus correction

The most important conceptual distinction in trend reading is the difference between impulse and corrective moves:

**Impulse move (the run):** The stronger movement in the direction of the trend. It tends to have larger candle bodies, fewer overlapping candles, and clearer closes in the trend direction. In a downtrend the impulse is down. In an uptrend the impulse is up.

**Correction (the pullback):** The weaker movement against the trend. It tends to have smaller candle bodies, more red-and-green mixing, and slower price progression. In a downtrend the correction is a bounce upward. In an uptrend the correction is a dip downward.

The ratio matters: in a healthy trend, impulse moves are meaningfully larger than corrective moves. If corrections start matching or exceeding the impulse moves in size or speed, the dominant side is losing control.

## What a healthy pullback looks like

A healthy pullback does not break the major swing structure.

In an uptrend, a pullback can dip, but it should ideally hold above the prior higher low. In a downtrend, a bounce can rise, but it should ideally fail below the prior lower high.

This is why pullbacks cannot be judged by color alone. A few green candles in a downtrend do not automatically mean reversal. They may only be temporary relief — the corrective portion of the impulse-correction rhythm — before sellers return and continue the trend.

A healthy pullback in a downtrend looks like:
- Green candles appearing after a red impulse sequence.
- These green candles are smaller than the red impulse candles.
- They overlap more with each other (slower movement).
- They fail to push above the last swing high.
- After the bounce, sellers resume and the price continues making lower lows.

## How candles reveal weak pullbacks

Weak pullbacks that are likely to be followed by trend continuation often show:

- **Smaller candle bodies** than the impulse move.
- **More overlapping candles** — price is not moving cleanly in the counter-trend direction.
- **Wicks that reject the counter-trend direction** — for example, in a downtrend, upper wicks on the green bouncing candles show sellers stepping in to reject higher prices.
- **Failure to close beyond the prior swing level** — the bounce cannot reclaim the last lower high.

These clues together suggest the counter-trend side is active but not strong enough to take control.

## When a pullback becomes a reversal warning

A pullback becomes more serious when it breaks structure.

In an uptrend, a pullback that makes a **lower low** is no longer a normal higher-low dip. The higher-low sequence has been broken. In a downtrend, a bounce that makes a **higher high** is no longer a normal lower-high bounce. The lower-high sequence has been broken.

That does not guarantee a full reversal, but it tells you the previous trend rhythm has changed. Refer to the trend change signals chapter for the three-step process of confirming whether a break leads to a genuine reversal or just a temporary disruption.

## Pullback versus trend change — the key test

The cleanest way to distinguish a pullback from a reversal is the swing point test:

**Pullback:** The corrective move fails to break the last swing point that defines the trend. The prior higher low holds in an uptrend. The prior lower high holds in a downtrend. The trend structure remains intact.

**Reversal warning:** The corrective move breaks the last defining swing point. In an uptrend, the higher low is broken. In a downtrend, the lower high is broken. The structure is now in question.

This is why you must identify and monitor the swing points of the active trend. They are the reference levels that tell you whether a correction is healthy or structural damage.

## Using pullbacks as context, not signals

A pullback tells you where price is — counter-trend territory. It does not by itself tell you whether to act.

The sequence for using a pullback properly:

1. Define the active trend direction.
2. Define the swing level that should hold if the trend is still valid.
3. Wait for price to approach that level.
4. Watch for candle behavior that shows momentum returning in the trend direction — smaller, weaker candles on the pullback followed by a clear impulse candle reclaiming trend direction.

Without steps 1 and 2, step 4 is just reacting to noise.

## Beginner mistake to avoid

Do not enter only because price "pulled back." A pullback is a location, not a signal.

Many beginners see a dip in an uptrend and immediately buy because "it's lower than before." But if the dip is actually breaking the prior higher low and forming a lower low, what looks like a buying opportunity is the early stage of a trend reversal.

First define the trend. Then define the swing level that should hold. Then wait for candles to show whether momentum is returning in the trend direction or structure is breaking.
