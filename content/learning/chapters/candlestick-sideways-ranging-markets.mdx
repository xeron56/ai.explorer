Not every chart is trending. Many charts spend long periods moving sideways. In the screenshots, this is shown as a cluster of red and green candles boxed inside a horizontal area — price making roughly the same highs and roughly the same lows repeatedly, without making meaningful progress in either direction.

A sideways market means neither buyers nor sellers have full control. Price may still move up and down, but it is not producing clean higher highs or lower lows.

## How to identify a sideways market

The screenshots label this clearly: price is making roughly the same highs, which shows a sideways and ranging market. Price is also making roughly the same lows, which confirms it.

Two parallel bands emerge — one at the top where price keeps stalling, and one at the bottom where price keeps bouncing. The distance between those bands is the range. Within it, candles alternate between red and green, and direction keeps reversing.

If you cannot identify clean higher highs with clean higher lows, and you cannot identify clean lower lows with clean lower highs, the market is likely ranging.

## What a range looks like

A range has two visible boundaries: a top where price repeatedly stalls, and a bottom where price repeatedly bounces.

Inside those boundaries, candles often alternate between red and green. Bodies may be smaller. Wicks may appear on both sides. This is why range movement often feels choppy and difficult to read as a clean trend.

The Apple screenshots show a period from late December 2022 to early January 2023 where price consolidated near the lows before a recovery. Within that zone, the candle pattern is mixed — some green sessions, some red sessions, no clean directional sequence.

## Why mixed candles matter

When strong buyers are in control, you usually see cleaner green continuation, shallow pullbacks, and higher closes. When strong sellers are in control, you usually see cleaner red continuation, weak bounces, and lower closes.

In a range, neither side can maintain control for long. Buyers win one candle, sellers win the next, and price rotates back toward the middle. This produces the mixed red-and-green appearance that is the visual hallmark of a ranging market.

## Price action psychology behind a range

A ranging market means both sides are active, but neither has enough conviction to create sustained continuation.

At the range top, buyers become less willing to chase higher prices and sellers become more willing to defend. At the range bottom, sellers become less willing to push lower and buyers become more willing to step in.

This is why range charts feel frustrating. A bullish candle near the middle can fail because it runs into the top boundary. A bearish candle near the middle can fail because it runs into the bottom boundary. The market is rotating between areas instead of trending cleanly.

A key point from the screenshots: in a sideways market, you can actually trade in both directions with confidence — buying near the bottom boundary and selling near the top boundary — because neither side has full control. This is the opposite of a trending market where you want to trade with the trend and not against it.

## The middle is usually noise

The middle of a range is where signals are weakest. Price has room to move in either direction, and small candles in the middle can be misleading in either direction.

The edges are more informative. At the top, you can watch whether buyers break through or sellers reject price back into the range. At the bottom, you can watch whether sellers break down or buyers defend the zone.

Candles at the edges of a range tell you something specific about whether the boundary is holding. Candles in the middle tell you mostly that neither side is doing anything decisive yet.

## How a range ends

A range ends when price breaks through a boundary and holds outside it.

The break itself is not enough. A candle can spike above resistance or below support and then fall back inside — a false breakout. A stronger sign of a genuine range break has three parts:

1. A clear **close** outside the boundary (not just a wick through it).
2. **Follow-through** candles that do not immediately return inside the range.
3. A **retest** where the old boundary holds from the new side — former resistance becomes support, or former support becomes resistance.

If the range top is at $134 and price closes convincingly above it, then pulls back to $134 and bounces upward, the range has likely ended with a bullish breakout.

## False breakout warnings

Many screenshots show why you should not trust every wick through a level. A wick above the range top means price traded there, but sellers pushed it back inside before the close. A wick below the range bottom means price traded lower, but buyers pushed it back inside.

Wicks through boundaries are common during ranges — they are how the market tests whether real participants are present at those levels. A wick that gets immediately rejected and pushes back inside is actually a sign the boundary is holding, not breaking.

## Range reading checklist

Ask these questions:

- Is price making clean higher highs and higher lows? (Uptrend)
- Is price making clean lower lows and lower highs? (Downtrend)
- Are candles mixed and overlapping with no clear directional sequence?
- Are reactions happening around the same top and bottom repeatedly?
- Did price close outside the boundaries, or only wick through them?

If the answers show overlap and repeated boundary reactions without directional progress, treat the chart as range-bound until price proves otherwise.

## Beginner mistake to avoid

Do not force a trend label onto a sideways chart. If structure is unclear, the correct answer is often consolidation.

Learning to say "this market is ranging right now" is a real and valuable skill. It keeps you from using trend-following logic in a market that is not trending, which is one of the most common ways traders lose money.
