Support and resistance are the first chart structures to learn after candle anatomy. They explain why price often reacts around the same area more than once, and they give you reference points for reading whether a move is meaningful or random.

The Apple chart in the screenshots shows this clearly: price fell, paused around a lower level, and later moved back above that area. That repeated reaction at the same price area is what makes a level worth marking and watching.

## What support means

Support is an area where buying pressure has previously appeared and been strong enough to stop or reverse a downward move.

It does not guarantee that price will rise again. It tells you the market has previously treated that area as attractive enough for buyers to respond. When price returns to support, the question becomes: are those buyers still there?

Candles near support often show lower wicks, smaller red bodies, or brief dips that quickly recover. Those signs suggest sellers are still active but their control may be weakening — the floor is being tested.

## What resistance means

Resistance is an area where selling pressure has previously appeared and been strong enough to stop or reverse an upward move.

It does not guarantee that price will fall. It tells you buyers previously struggled to push through that zone with enough force.

Candles near resistance often show upper wicks, failed breakout attempts, or a strong green candle that quickly loses follow-through on the next session. Those signs suggest buyers are present but sellers are defending.

## Levels are zones, not magic numbers

One of the most common beginner errors is treating a support or resistance level as a single exact price.

The Apple screenshots point to specific numbers — $126.05, $130.24, $142.21 — but real chart reading should think in zones around those numbers. A level can span the range of nearby candle bodies, wicks, and closes that reacted in the same area. If multiple candles reacted around $130, then the useful zone is something like $129 to $131, not just $130.00.

Why zones? Because different participants act at slightly different prices. Some buyers step in at $129.80, some at $130.10, some at $130.40. Their collective buying creates a zone of support, not a precise line.

Use wicks to define the outer edge of a zone and candle bodies to define the inner edge. Bodies show where price was accepted (traded for multiple sessions). Wicks show where price explored but rejected quickly.

## How to mark a useful level

A good level meets at least two of these criteria:

1. **Multiple reactions.** Price touched or approached the area at least twice and showed a visible change in behavior each time. One touch is a data point. Two or more touches create a pattern.

2. **Clear behavioral change.** Price either reversed, stalled significantly, or broke through in a meaningful way. A level matters more when it caused something visible to happen — a sharp rejection, a consolidation pause, or a breakout followed by continuation.

3. **Recent.** A level from six months ago on a daily chart carries less weight than one from two weeks ago, because market conditions change and the participants who acted at that price may have different positions now.

4. **Clean.** Prefer levels where the candle reactions are relatively obvious and not surrounded by too much noise. If you have to squint or cherry-pick candles to justify the level, it is probably not clean enough to be useful.

## Break and retest

When price breaks above resistance and then pulls back, the former resistance area can become support. When price breaks below support and then bounces, the former support can become resistance.

This role-reversal behavior happens because traders adapt. Buyers who were stopped at a resistance zone earlier may now view that same area — once price proves it through — as confirmation of strength and a place to add to their position. Their buying is what turns the old ceiling into a new floor.

Similarly, sellers who bought near old support and got trapped when price broke through may now view the same area as a place to sell when price returns. Their selling turns the former floor into a new ceiling.

Break and retest is one of the most practical patterns in chart reading because it transforms a prior reaction point into a forward-looking reference.

## False breakout warnings

Not every push through a level is a real breakout. Wicks through a level show the market explored that territory but did not accept it.

A false breakout is when price crosses a level, closes back inside the prior range, and then reverses. These are particularly common at range tops and bottoms where traders are watching the same obvious level.

Signs of a stronger, genuine breakout:
- A candle **closes** convincingly outside the level (not just a wick).
- Follow-through candles appear that do not immediately return inside.
- On a pullback to the broken level, candles show support or resistance from the new side.

If price breaks above resistance but immediately falls back below within one or two candles, treat it as a potential false breakout until proven otherwise.

## Beginner mistake to avoid

Do not draw too many levels. If every candle high and low has a line drawn through it, no level is meaningful because everything becomes noise.

Mark only the areas that clearly changed price behavior — visible reversals, obvious stalls, meaningful breaks. Aim for three to five key levels on any chart view, not thirty.

A good level should help you answer one question: if price returns here, what reaction would prove buyers or sellers are still active at this area?
