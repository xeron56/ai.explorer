The gradient $\nabla f(x)$ points in the direction of steepest *increase* of
$f$ at $x$. So gradient descent steps the opposite way:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\, \nabla f(x_t).
$$

The step size $\eta$ has to play nice with the local curvature. If the Hessian
$\nabla^2 f$ has largest eigenvalue $L$, then any $\eta < 2/L$ guarantees
decrease for quadratic $f$. The "best" rate in the convex case is achieved at
$\eta = 2 / (L + \mu)$, where $\mu$ is the smallest eigenvalue.

What this means geometrically: gradient descent is a *first-order* method, so
it's blind to anisotropy. Conditioning the problem — preconditioning,
normalization, careful parameterization — pays for itself many times over.
