  function money(x) { var sign = x < 0 ? "-" : ""; return sign + "Tk " + Math.abs(x).toLocaleString("en-US", { maximumFractionDigits: 0 }); }
  function pct(x) { return x.toFixed(2).replace(/\.00$/, "") + "%"; }
  function bondPricePct(couponPct, yieldPct, years) {
    var c = couponPct;
    var y = yieldPct / 100;
    if (Math.abs(y) < 0.000001) return c * years + 100;
    return c * (1 - Math.pow(1 + y, -years)) / y + 100 / Math.pow(1 + y, years);
  }
  function modifiedDuration(couponPct, yieldPct, years) {
    var y = yieldPct / 100;
    var price = bondPricePct(couponPct, yieldPct, years);
    if (price <= 0) return 0;
    var weighted = 0;
    for (var t = 1; t <= years; t++) {
      var cf = couponPct + (t === years ? 100 : 0);
      weighted += t * cf / Math.pow(1 + y, t);
    }
    var macaulay = weighted / price;
    return macaulay / (1 + y);
  }
  function getState() {
    var face = num("face");
    var qty = num("qty");
    var coupon = num("coupon");
    var years = num("years");
    var clean = num("clean");
    var purchase = num("purchase");
    var accrued = num("accrued");
    var payfreq = num("payfreq");
    var marketYield = Number(els.marketYield.value || 0);
    var shock = num("rateShock");
    var totalFace = face * qty;
    var annualCoupon = totalFace * coupon / 100;
    var couponPayment = annualCoupon / payfreq;
    var cleanValue = totalFace * clean / 100;
    var dirtyValue = cleanValue + accrued;
    var purchaseValue = totalFace * purchase / 100;
    var capitalGl = cleanValue - purchaseValue;
    var approxYtm = ((coupon + (100 - clean) / years) / ((100 + clean) / 2)) * 100;
    var modelPrice = bondPricePct(coupon, marketYield, years);
    var duration = modifiedDuration(coupon, marketYield, years);
    return { face: face, qty: qty, coupon: coupon, years: years, clean: clean, purchase: purchase, accrued: accrued, payfreq: payfreq, marketYield: marketYield, shock: shock, totalFace: totalFace, annualCoupon: annualCoupon, couponPayment: couponPayment, cleanValue: cleanValue, dirtyValue: dirtyValue, purchaseValue: purchaseValue, capitalGl: capitalGl, approxYtm: approxYtm, modelPrice: modelPrice, duration: duration };
  }

# Bonds for Complete Beginners

Imagine your friend **Rahim** owns a small restaurant.

One day Rahim says:

> “I want to open a second branch, but I need **100,000 taka**. I do not want to sell part of my business. Can you lend me money? I will pay you interest every year, and after 5 years I will return your full money.”

You agree.

You give Rahim **100,000**.

Rahim promises:

```text
Loan amount: 100,000
Interest rate: 10% per year
Time: 5 years
Interest payment: 10,000 per year
Principal repayment after 5 years: 100,000
```

This is the basic idea of a **bond**.

A bond is a formal version of this loan. When you buy a bond, you are not buying ownership. You are lending money.

<BondCashFlowTimeline />

Before we go deeper, keep this simple sentence in your mind:

> **A bond is a loan with a schedule.**

The schedule says who borrowed the money, how much interest they will pay, when they will pay it, and when they will return the principal.

This article is educational only. It is not personal financial advice.

---

## 1. What is a bond?

A **bond** is a loan given by investors to an organization.

The borrower can be a government, bank, company, corporation, or municipality.

The lender is you, the investor.

So when you buy a bond:

```text
You = lender
Bond issuer = borrower
Coupon = interest
Maturity = loan ending date
Face value = principal amount
```

Real-life example:

```text
You buy a government bond.
The government borrows money from you.
The government pays you interest.
At maturity, the government returns your principal.
```

The easiest way to understand bonds is to compare them with normal loans.

When a bank gives a home loan, the bank is the lender and the homeowner is the borrower.

When you buy a bond, you are acting like the lender.

The bond issuer is the borrower.

---

## 2. Rahim’s Restaurant Bond

Rahim wants to raise money for his second restaurant.

He creates a bond:

```text
Bond name: Rahim Restaurant Bond
Face value: 1,000 per bond
Coupon rate: 10%
Maturity: 5 years
```

This means each bond has a principal value of **1,000**.

You buy **100 bonds**.

Math:

```text
Total investment = 100 bonds × 1,000
                 = 100,000
```

Rahim pays 10% interest every year.

```text
Annual coupon income = 100,000 × 10%
                     = 100,000 × 0.10
                     = 10,000
```

So every year, you receive **10,000**.

After 5 years, Rahim returns your original **100,000**.

Your cash flow looks like this:

```text
Year 0: You pay 100,000
Year 1: You receive 10,000 interest
Year 2: You receive 10,000 interest
Year 3: You receive 10,000 interest
Year 4: You receive 10,000 interest
Year 5: You receive 10,000 interest + 100,000 principal
```

Total received:

```text
Interest received = 10,000 × 5
                  = 50,000

Principal returned = 100,000

Total received = 150,000
```

Your profit is:

```text
Profit = Total received - Original investment
       = 150,000 - 100,000
       = 50,000
```

That is how a simple bond works if you hold it until maturity and the issuer pays everything promised.

```text
Annual interest = 100,000 × 8%
                = 100,000 × 0.08
                = 8,000
```

You receive **8,000 per year**.

## 3. Bond vs stock

This is very important.

When you buy a **stock**, you become part-owner of a company.

When you buy a **bond**, you become a lender.

```text
Stock = ownership
Bond = loan
```

If Rahim sells you shares of his restaurant, you own part of the restaurant.

If Rahim sells you bonds, you do not own the restaurant. He simply owes you money.

| Question | Stock investor | Bond investor |
|---|---|---|
| What do you own? | Part of the business | A promise to repay debt |
| Main goal | Company growth and rising share price | Interest income and principal repayment |
| Return source | Dividends and price gains | Coupon income and possible price gains |
| Risk style | Usually higher | Usually lower than stocks, but not zero |
| Best mental model | Business ownership | Lending money |

A stock investor thinks:

```text
I hope the company grows and share price increases.
```

A bond investor thinks:

```text
I want regular interest and my principal back safely.
```

Stocks usually have higher risk and higher possible return.

Bonds usually have lower risk and more predictable income.

But “usually” is important. A weak company bond can be much riskier than a strong company stock. You always need to look at the issuer.

## 4. Main bond keywords

Bond language feels difficult at first because finance uses special words for simple ideas. Let’s translate them.

### Face value

**Face value** is the principal amount of the bond.

Example:

```text
Face value = 1,000
```

This means one bond has a principal value of 1,000.

If you buy 50 bonds:

```text
Total face value = 50 × 1,000
                 = 50,000
```

At maturity, you usually get back the face value if the issuer does not default.

So if you hold until maturity:

```text
Principal repayment = 50,000
```

Real-life meaning:

> Face value is the amount the borrower promises to repay at the end.

---

### Coupon rate

**Coupon rate** is the interest rate of the bond.

Example:

```text
Face value = 1,000
Coupon rate = 8.50%
```

Interest per bond per year:

```text
Annual coupon = 1,000 × 8.50%
              = 1,000 × 0.085
              = 85
```

If you own 50 bonds:

```text
Annual coupon = 85 × 50
              = 4,250
```

So coupon means **bond interest income**.

Real-life meaning:

> Coupon is like the rent your money earns while it is lent out.

---

### Maturity

**Maturity** is the date when the bond ends.

Example:

```text
Maturity = 15-Dec-2029
```

At maturity, the borrower returns the principal.

If you own:

```text
50 bonds
Face value = 1,000
```

Then at maturity:

```text
You receive = 50 × 1,000
            = 50,000
```

Maturity is like the loan return date.

A 1-year bond is a short loan.

A 10-year bond is a long loan.

Longer loans usually have more uncertainty.

---

### Issuer

**Issuer** means the organization that borrows money by selling the bond.

Examples:

```text
Government
Bank
Company
Corporation
Municipality
```

If the government issues a bond, it is a **government bond**.

If a company issues a bond, it is a **corporate bond**.

The issuer is important because the bond is only as strong as the borrower.

Before buying any bond, ask:

```text
Who owes me the money?
Can they really pay me back?
```

## 5. Types of bonds

### Government bonds

A government bond is issued by the government.

Examples:

```text
Bangladesh Government Treasury Bond
US Treasury Bond
```

Government bonds are usually considered safer than corporate bonds because governments often have stronger ability to collect taxes, manage budgets, and support public debt.

But safer does not mean risk-free.

Government bonds can still have inflation risk, interest-rate risk, and currency risk.

---

### Corporate bonds

A corporate bond is issued by a company.

Examples:

```text
Bank bond
Power company bond
Telecom company bond
Manufacturing company bond
```

Corporate bonds usually pay higher coupon than government bonds because companies are usually riskier than governments.

Simple idea:

```text
More safety = lower return
More risk = higher return
```

But a high coupon is not automatically good.

A high coupon may be the market’s warning sign:

> “This borrower is risky, so investors demand extra return.”

---

### Other beginner categories

You may also hear these names:

| Bond type | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Treasury bond | Bond issued by a national government |
| Municipal bond | Bond issued by a city, state, or local government |
| Investment-grade bond | Bond from a borrower considered relatively stronger |
| High-yield bond | Bond from a riskier borrower; sometimes called junk bond |
| Zero-coupon bond | Bond that pays no regular coupon but is sold at a discount |
| Floating-rate bond | Coupon changes with a reference interest rate |

For beginners, do not try to memorize every bond type on day one.

Start with the main question:

```text
Who borrowed the money, how much will they pay, and when will they repay principal?
```

## 6. How bond income works

Suppose you buy this bond:

```text
Face value = 1,000
Quantity = 20
Coupon = 9.25%
```

First find total face value:

```text
Total face value = Face value × Quantity
                 = 1,000 × 20
                 = 20,000
```

Now calculate yearly coupon income:

```text
Annual coupon income = Total face value × Coupon rate
                     = 20,000 × 9.25%
                     = 20,000 × 0.0925
                     = 1,850
```

So you earn **1,850 per year**.

If coupon is paid twice per year:

```text
Each payment = 1,850 ÷ 2
             = 925
```

If coupon is paid every 6 months, you receive **925 every 6 months**.

The formula is:

```text
Annual coupon income = Face value × Quantity × Coupon rate
```

Example:

```text
1,000 × 20 × 9.25%
= 1,850
```

```text
Total face value = 70 × 1,000
                 = 70,000

Annual coupon income = 70,000 × 7%
                     = 70,000 × 0.07
                     = 4,900
```

You receive **4,900 per year**.

## 8. Bond price is not always 1,000

Many beginners think:

> “If face value is 1,000, bond price must always be 1,000.”

Not true.

Bond price changes in the market.

A bond can trade at:

```text
Below face value
At face value
Above face value
```

Bond prices are often shown as a percentage of **100**.

Example:

```text
Clean price = 98.75

```

This means:

```text

             = 0.9875 × 1,000
             = 987.50
```

So one bond costs **987.50**, not 1,000.

If clean price is 101.20:

```text
Market price = 101.20% of 1,000
             = 1.0120 × 1,000
             = 1,012
```

So the bond is trading above face value.

The clean price quote is like a shortcut.

```text
98.75 means 98.75% of face value
101.20 means 101.20% of face value
```

## 9. Discount, par, and premium

### Discount bond

A bond trades at a **discount** when price is below 100.

Example:

```text
Clean price = 98.75
Face value = 1,000
```

Price:

```text
Price = 98.75% × 1,000
      = 987.50
```

You pay **987.50** now, but at maturity you may receive **1,000**.

That extra **12.50** is a capital gain if held to maturity and the issuer pays.

Beginner meaning:

> Discount bond = you buy the bond for less than the principal repayment amount.

---

### Par bond

A bond trades at **par** when price is 100.

Example:

```text
Clean price = 100.00
Face value = 1,000
Price = 1,000
```

You pay exactly face value.

Beginner meaning:

> Par bond = the bond price equals the principal amount.

---

### Premium bond

A bond trades at a **premium** when price is above 100.

Example:

```text
Clean price = 101.20
Face value = 1,000
```

Price:

```text
Price = 101.20% × 1,000
      = 1,012
```

You pay more than face value.

Why would someone pay more?

Because the coupon may be attractive compared with current market rates.

Imagine old bond coupon is 12%, but new bonds only pay 8%. Investors may be willing to pay extra for the old higher coupon.

## 10. Clean price and dirty price

This is one of the most confusing beginner topics.

A bond has two prices:

```text
Clean price
Dirty price
```

### Clean price

Clean price is the quoted bond price without accrued interest.

Example:

```text
Clean price = 98.75
```

This is the market quote.

When people talk about bond price, they often mean clean price.

---

### Accrued interest

Accrued interest is interest already earned since the last coupon date but not yet paid.

Imagine Rahim’s bond pays interest every 6 months.

```text
Last coupon date = January 1
Next coupon date = July 1
You buy the bond = April 1
```

The seller held the bond from January 1 to April 1.

So the seller earned about 3 months of interest.

But the next coupon payment on July 1 will go to the new buyer.

To be fair, the buyer pays the seller for the interest already earned.

That unpaid earned interest is called **accrued interest**.

---

### Dirty price

Dirty price is the actual price including accrued interest.

Formula:

```text
Dirty price = Clean price + Accrued interest portion
```

Example:

```text
Clean price = 98.75
Accrued interest portion = 2.12

Dirty price = 98.75 + 2.12
            = 100.87
```

So:

```text
Clean price = quoted price
Dirty price = actual settlement price
```

For beginners, remember:

```text
You see clean price.
You pay dirty price.
```

Real-life analogy:

> Clean price is the menu price. Dirty price is the final bill after adding the earned interest that belongs to the seller.

---

## 11. Bond value calculation

Suppose:

```text
Dirty price = 100.87
Face value = 1,000
Quantity = 50
```

First calculate total face value:

```text
Total face value = 1,000 × 50
                 = 50,000
```

Now calculate dirty value:

```text
Dirty value = 100.87% × 50,000
            = 1.0087 × 50,000
            = 50,435
```

So the bond’s current value is **50,435**.

This is why bond dashboards often use dirty value. It is closer to the actual settlement value.

But your clean market value would be:

```text
Clean value = 98.75% × 50,000
            = 0.9875 × 50,000
            = 49,375
```

And accrued interest would explain the difference between clean value and dirty value.

## 12. Capital gain and capital loss in bonds

A bond can make money in two ways:

```text
1. Coupon income
2. Capital gain
```

Coupon income is interest.

Capital gain is price profit.

Example:

```text
You bought bond at 97.50
Current clean price = 98.75
```

Gain:

```text
Price gain = 98.75 - 97.50
           = 1.25
```

Because bond price is quoted per 100, this means **1.25%**.

If total face value is 50,000:

```text
Capital gain = 1.25% × 50,000
             = 0.0125 × 50,000
             = 625
```

So your bond has a capital gain of **625**.

If price falls below your purchase price, then you have a capital loss.

Example:

```text
Bought at 100
Current price = 97
```

Capital loss:

```text
Capital loss = 3% of face value
```

If face value is 50,000:

```text
Capital loss = 3% × 50,000
             = 1,500
```

## 13. Yield to maturity, or YTM

**YTM** means **Yield to Maturity**.

It is the expected yearly return if you hold the bond until maturity and all payments happen as promised.

YTM considers:

```text
Coupon income
Current price
Face value repayment
Time until maturity
```

Coupon rate and YTM are not the same.

### Coupon rate

Coupon rate is fixed.

Example:

```text
Coupon = 8.50%
```

This tells how much interest the bond pays based on face value.

### YTM

YTM changes when bond price changes.

Example:

```text
Coupon = 8.50%
Bond price = 98.75
YTM = around 8.6% to 9.0%, depending on maturity and payment timing
```

Why can YTM be higher than coupon?

Because you buy below face value.

You pay **987.50** for a bond that may return **1,000** at maturity. That extra gain increases your return.

Simple rule:

```text
If bond price < 100, YTM is usually higher than coupon.
If bond price = 100, YTM is close to coupon.
If bond price > 100, YTM is usually lower than coupon.
```

Approximate YTM formula for beginners:

```text
Approx. YTM =
Annual coupon + yearly gain/loss toward face value
--------------------------------------------------
Average of current price and face value
```

Example:

```text
Face value = 1,000
Current price = 950
Coupon = 80 per year
Years to maturity = 5
```

Step 1: find yearly gain toward face value.

```text
Gain at maturity = 1,000 - 950
                 = 50

Yearly gain = 50 ÷ 5
            = 10
```

Step 2: add annual coupon.

```text
Annual coupon + yearly gain = 80 + 10
                            = 90
```

Step 3: divide by average price.

```text
Average price = (950 + 1,000) ÷ 2
              = 975

Approx. YTM = 90 ÷ 975
            = 9.23%
```

So the bond pays an 8% coupon, but your approximate YTM is about **9.23%** because you bought it below face value.

## 14. Why bond price changes

Bond price changes mainly because of interest rates and risk.

### Interest-rate effect

Suppose Rahim issued an old bond:

```text
Old bond coupon = 8%
```

Later, new bonds come to market:

```text
New bond coupon = 10%
```

Now investors prefer the new 10% bond.

So the old 8% bond becomes less attractive.

To sell the old 8% bond, its price must fall.

That price fall makes its effective yield more attractive.

Simple rule:

```text
Interest rates go up → old bond prices go down
Interest rates go down → old bond prices go up
```

<BondPriceSeeSaw />

### Credit-risk effect

Bond prices also change if investors become more worried about the issuer.

Example:

Rahim’s restaurant sales are strong. People trust Rahim. His bond price may stay stable or rise.

But if Rahim’s restaurant starts losing money, investors worry that Rahim may not pay. His bond price may fall.

So bond prices move because of two big forces:

```text
Market interest rates
Issuer safety
```

## 15. Bond pricing formula, explained simply

A bond is worth the present value of its future cash flows.

That sounds complicated, so translate it:

> A bond’s fair price is today’s value of all future coupons plus today’s value of the final principal repayment.

Formula idea:

```text
Bond price = Present value of coupons + Present value of principal
```

Present value means future money adjusted back to today.

If the required return is high, future money is worth less today.

If the required return is low, future money is worth more today.

Simple present value formula:

```text
Present value = Future cash flow ÷ (1 + market yield)^year
```

Example:

```text
Future cash flow = 10,000
Market yield = 10%
Year = 1

Present value = 10,000 ÷ (1 + 0.10)^1
              = 10,000 ÷ 1.10
              = 9,090.91
```

Why is 10,000 next year worth only 9,090.91 today?

Because if you invest 9,090.91 today at 10%, it becomes about 10,000 after one year.

```text
9,090.91 × 1.10 = 10,000
```

That is the heart of bond pricing.

## 16. Duration

**Duration** measures how sensitive a bond is to interest-rate changes.

Example:

```text
Modified duration = 2.8 years
```

Simple meaning:

```text
If interest rates rise by 1%, bond price may fall about 2.8%.
If interest rates fall by 1%, bond price may rise about 2.8%.
```

Duration is not exactly maturity, but they are related.

Longer maturity bonds usually have higher duration.

Higher duration means higher interest-rate risk.

Example:

```text
Short bond duration = less price movement
Long bond duration = more price movement
```

Duration estimate formula:

```text
Approx. price change % = -Modified duration × Interest rate change
```

Example:

```text
Modified duration = 4
Interest rates rise = 1%

Approx. price change = -4 × 1%
                     = -4%
```

If your bond value is 50,000:

```text
Estimated loss = 4% × 50,000
               = 2,000
```

So the value may fall by about **2,000**.

Beginner warning:

> Duration is an estimate. It is most useful for small rate changes. It is not a perfect prediction.

## 17. Bond risks beginners should know

Many beginners think bonds are completely safe.

Bonds are usually safer than stocks, but they still have risks.

<BondRiskMap />

### Default risk

Default risk means the issuer may fail to pay interest or principal.

Example:

```text
Rahim’s restaurant business fails.
Rahim cannot pay coupon.
Rahim cannot return principal.
```

That is default risk.

Government bonds usually have lower default risk than corporate bonds.

Corporate bonds usually have higher default risk.

But every issuer must be judged carefully.

---

### Interest-rate risk

If market interest rates rise, your bond price may fall.

Example:

```text
You hold 8% bond.
Market offers new 10% bond.
Your 8% bond becomes less attractive.
Price falls.
```

This matters most if you sell before maturity.

If you hold to maturity and the issuer does not default, you may still receive face value at the end.

But your bond’s market value can move along the way.

---

### Liquidity risk

Liquidity means how easily you can sell.

If many buyers exist, the bond is liquid.

If few buyers exist, the bond is illiquid.

Illiquid bonds may be difficult to sell quickly at a good price.

Real-life analogy:

> A popular phone model is easy to resell. A rare item with few buyers may need a discount. Bonds can work the same way.

---

### Inflation risk

Inflation reduces purchasing power.

Example:

```text
Your bond pays 8%.
Inflation is 10%.
```

Your simple real return idea:

```text
Real return ≈ Bond return - Inflation
Real return ≈ 8% - 10%
Real return ≈ -2%
```

You earned money, but your buying power decreased.

Real-life example:

If your coupon income buys 100 kg of rice today but only 90 kg next year, inflation has reduced your purchasing power.

---

### Reinvestment risk

If your bond pays coupon, you may need to reinvest that coupon.

If future rates are lower, you may not be able to reinvest at the same return.

Example:

```text
Your bond pays 10%.
Later market rate falls to 6%.
Your coupon money can only be reinvested at 6%.
```

That is reinvestment risk.

This is one reason YTM is an estimate. It often assumes coupons can be reinvested at the same yield, which may not happen in real life.

---

## 18. Why people buy bonds

People buy bonds for several reasons.

### Regular income

Bonds can provide predictable coupon income.

Example:

```text
You invest 100,000 in a 10% coupon bond.
You may receive 10,000 per year.
```

This is useful for people who want cash flow.

Examples:

```text
Retired person seeking income
Investor balancing stock risk
Institution matching future payments
Business parking funds for a known date
```

---

### Lower volatility than stocks

Stock prices can move sharply.

Bond prices usually move less, especially high-quality short-term bonds.

So bonds can make a portfolio more stable.

But this depends on bond quality and duration.

A long-term risky corporate bond can still be volatile.

---

### Diversification

If you own only stocks, your portfolio may be very risky.

If you own both stocks and bonds, risk can be more balanced.

Example:

```text
Equity = growth
Bond = income/stability
Cash = flexibility
```

Bonds are not meant to make every portfolio risk-free.

They are meant to add a different return source and risk profile.

---

### Principal return at maturity

If the issuer does not default, bonds return face value at maturity.

This makes bonds easier to plan around than stocks.

Example:

```text
Goal: pay university fee in 3 years
Possible tool: high-quality bond maturing around that date
```

The maturity date can help match your investment with a future need.

## 19. How to read your bond dashboard

A bond dashboard may show columns like:

```text
ISIN / Name
Type
Clean price
Face value
Coupon%
Maturity
Qty units
Avg purchase
Accrued int.
Dirty value
YTM%
Coupon income
Capital G/L
Holding%
```

Let’s explain using one example.

Suppose your bond row says:

```text
Bond: BD001GVT26
Type: Treasury
Clean price: 98.75
Face value: 1,000
Coupon: 8.50%
Maturity: 15-Dec-2029
Qty: 50
Avg purchase: 97.50
Accrued interest: 2,125
Dirty value: 50,435
YTM: 8.62%
Coupon income: 4,250
Capital G/L: +625
```

Meaning:

You own 50 units of a government treasury bond.

Each bond has face value 1,000.

```text
Total face value = 50 × 1,000
                 = 50,000
```

The coupon is 8.50%.

```text
Annual coupon = 50,000 × 8.50%
              = 4,250
```

You bought around 97.50.

```text
Purchase value = 97.50% × 50,000
               = 48,750
```

Now clean price is 98.75.

```text
Current clean value = 98.75% × 50,000
                    = 49,375
```

Capital gain:

```text
Capital gain = 49,375 - 48,750
             = 625
```

Accrued interest is 2,125.

So a simple dirty value estimate is:

```text
Dirty value = Clean value + Accrued interest
            = 49,375 + 2,125
            = 51,500
```

But your dashboard may use a dirty price of about 100.87:

```text
Dirty value = 100.87% × 50,000
            = 50,435
```

The exact number can depend on settlement date, day-count convention, coupon schedule, rounding, taxes, and platform methodology.

The beginner lesson is:

```text
Clean price shows market price.
Accrued interest shows unpaid earned coupon.
Dirty value shows a more realistic settlement value.
```

## 20. A full real-life bond story

Let’s continue Rahim’s story.

Rahim opens his second restaurant. He issues 100 bonds.

```text
Total money Rahim wants = 100,000
Face value per bond = 1,000
Number of bonds = 100
Coupon = 10%
Maturity = 5 years
```

You buy all 100 bonds.

Rahim receives:

```text
100 × 1,000 = 100,000
```

Every year Rahim pays:

```text
100,000 × 10% = 10,000
```

Now after 2 years, interest rates in the market change.

New restaurant bonds now pay 12%.

Your bond pays only 10%.

A new investor says:

> “Why should I buy your 10% bond when new bonds pay 12%?”

So if you want to sell your bond before maturity, you may need to sell it at a lower price.

Maybe your bond price falls to 95.

That means:

```text
Market price = 95% of 100,000
             = 95,000
```

If you sell now:

```text
Capital loss = 95,000 - 100,000
             = -5,000
```

But if you do not sell and Rahim does not default, you still receive coupons and principal at maturity.

Now imagine the opposite case.

Market rates fall to 7%.

Your bond pays 10%.

Now your bond is attractive.

People want your bond because it pays more than new bonds.

Your bond price may rise to 106.

```text
Market price = 106% of 100,000
             = 106,000
```

If you sell now:

```text
Capital gain = 106,000 - 100,000
             = 6,000
```

So bond price moves even though coupon is fixed.

The coupon stays 10%, but market value changes.

## 21. Bond return if held to maturity

Suppose you buy a bond at face value:

```text
Investment = 100,000
Coupon = 10%
Maturity = 5 years
```

You receive:

```text
Yearly coupon = 10,000
Total coupon over 5 years = 50,000
Principal at maturity = 100,000
```

Total received:

```text
150,000
```

Profit:

```text
50,000
```

This is simple.

Now suppose you buy the same bond at discount:

```text
Face value = 100,000
Purchase price = 95,000
Coupon = 10%
Maturity = 5 years
```

Coupon is still based on face value:

```text
Annual coupon = 100,000 × 10%
              = 10,000
```

At maturity you receive:

```text
Principal = 100,000
```

You paid only 95,000 but receive 100,000 at maturity.

Extra capital gain:

```text
100,000 - 95,000 = 5,000
```

Total coupon:

```text
10,000 × 5 = 50,000
```

Total profit:

```text
Coupon profit + Capital gain
= 50,000 + 5,000
= 55,000
```

This is why buying below face value increases your yield.

## 22. Bond return if sold before maturity

If you sell before maturity, your return depends on market price.

Example:

```text
You bought bond for 100,000
You received coupon for 2 years
Coupon = 10,000 per year
Total coupon received = 20,000
You sell bond for 95,000
```

Total received:

```text
Coupon received + Sale price
= 20,000 + 95,000
= 115,000
```

Profit:

```text
115,000 - 100,000
= 15,000
```

Even though you sold at a capital loss of 5,000, coupon income made total return positive.

Now another example:

```text
You bought bond for 100,000
You received coupon for 1 year = 10,000
You sell bond for 85,000
```

Total received:

```text
10,000 + 85,000 = 95,000
```

Profit/loss:

```text
95,000 - 100,000 = -5,000
```

So selling early can create a loss if price falls too much.

## 23. Beginner mental model: bond as rental income

Think of a bond like a rental property, but instead of rent, you receive coupon.

Suppose you buy an apartment for 100,000 and rent it for 10,000 per year.

```text
Apartment price = 100,000
Annual rent = 10,000
Rental yield = 10%
```

Bond is similar:

```text
Bond face value = 100,000
Annual coupon = 10,000
Coupon rate = 10%
```

But there is one big difference.

Apartment price can rise or fall.

Bond price can also rise or fall.

But if you hold a good bond until maturity, the issuer should return the face value.

A rental property does not have a fixed maturity date when someone must buy it back from you at face value.

That is why bond maturity is powerful for planning.

## 24. The most important formulas

Here is your beginner formula sheet.

### Total face value

```text
Total face value = Face value × Quantity
```

Example:

```text
1,000 × 50 = 50,000
```

Real-life meaning:

> This is the total principal amount behind your bond position.

---

### Annual coupon income

```text
Annual coupon income = Total face value × Coupon rate
```

Example:

```text
50,000 × 8.50% = 4,250
```

Real-life meaning:

> This is the interest income you expect in one year before taxes and fees.

---

### Coupon payment amount

```text
Each coupon payment = Annual coupon income ÷ Number of payments per year
```

Example:

```text
Annual coupon = 4,250
Payments per year = 2

Each payment = 4,250 ÷ 2
             = 2,125
```

Real-life meaning:

> If the bond pays every 6 months, you receive half the annual coupon each time.

---

### Clean market value

```text
Clean value = Clean price% × Total face value
```

Example:

```text
98.75% × 50,000 = 49,375
```

Real-life meaning:

> This is the market value before accrued interest.

---

### Dirty value

```text
Dirty value = Dirty price% × Total face value
```

Example:

```text
100.87% × 50,000 = 50,435
```

Or:

```text
Dirty value = Clean value + Accrued interest
```

Real-life meaning:

> This is closer to what changes hands at settlement.

---

### Capital gain/loss

```text
Capital G/L = Current clean value - Purchase value
```

Example:

```text
Current clean value = 49,375
Purchase value = 48,750

Capital G/L = 49,375 - 48,750
            = 625
```

Real-life meaning:

> This is your price profit or loss before considering coupons.

---

### Total bond gain

```text
Total bond gain = Coupon income + Capital gain/loss
```

Example:

```text
Coupon income = 4,250
Capital gain = 625

Total gain = 4,875
```

Real-life meaning:

> This combines income and price movement.

---

### Real return approximation

```text
Real return ≈ Bond return - Inflation
```

Example:

```text
Bond return = 8%
Inflation = 10%

Real return ≈ -2%
```

Real-life meaning:

> You may earn money but still lose buying power if inflation is higher.

---

### Duration estimate

```text
Approx. price change % = -Modified duration × Interest rate change
```

Example:

```text
Modified duration = 3
Rates rise = 1%

Approx. price change = -3 × 1%
                     = -3%
```

Real-life meaning:

> Duration helps estimate interest-rate risk.

---

## 25. Common beginner mistakes

### Mistake 1: Thinking coupon equals total return

Coupon is not always total return.

Total return includes:

```text
Coupon income
Capital gain/loss
Accrued interest
Taxes/fees if any
```

A bond with an 8% coupon can produce more or less than 8% depending on purchase price and sale price.

---

### Mistake 2: Thinking bonds cannot lose money

Bonds can lose money if:

```text
You sell before maturity at a lower price
Issuer defaults
Inflation is higher than coupon
Interest rates rise sharply
Fees/taxes reduce return
```

Bonds are usually calmer than stocks, but not risk-free.

---

### Mistake 3: Ignoring maturity

A 1-year bond and a 10-year bond are very different.

Longer maturity usually means more price risk.

If rates rise, the long bond usually moves more.

---

### Mistake 4: Ignoring issuer quality

A high coupon may look attractive.

But ask:

```text
Can the issuer really pay?
Is the company strong?
Is the government reliable?
Why is the coupon high?
```

Higher coupon often means higher risk.

---

### Mistake 5: Confusing clean price and dirty price

A beginner may see:

```text
Clean price = 98.75
```

And think that is the full amount paid.

But actual settlement may include accrued interest.

Remember:

```text
Clean price = quote
Dirty price = quote + accrued interest
```

## 26. Simple checklist before buying a bond

Before buying a bond, ask:

```text
Who is the issuer?
Is it government or corporate?
What is the coupon rate?
What is the maturity date?
What is the current clean price?
What is the dirty price or accrued interest?
What is the YTM?
How often does it pay coupon?
Can I sell it easily?
What happens if interest rates rise?
What is the default risk?
What is the inflation risk?
Does this bond match my goal date?
```

For complete beginners, these five are most important:

```text
Issuer
Coupon
Maturity
Price
YTM
```

If you understand those five, most bond dashboards become less scary.

## 27. How to think like a careful bond investor

A careful bond investor does not only ask:

```text
How much coupon will I get?
```

A careful bond investor asks:

```text
Who is paying me?
When will I get my money back?
What price am I paying today?
What yield am I actually earning?
What can go wrong?
```

This mindset protects you from chasing high coupons without understanding risk.

A bond is a promise.

Your job is to understand the promise and judge whether the borrower can keep it.

## 28. Final simple summary

A bond is a loan.

You give money to an issuer.

The issuer pays you interest.

At maturity, the issuer returns your principal.

The main bond terms are:

```text
Face value = principal amount
Coupon = interest rate
Maturity = ending date
Clean price = quoted market price
Dirty price = price including accrued interest
YTM = expected return if held to maturity
Capital gain/loss = price profit or loss
Duration = interest-rate sensitivity
```

The easiest formula:

```text
Annual coupon income = Face value × Quantity × Coupon%
```

Example:

```text
1,000 × 50 × 8.50%
= 4,250
```

The easiest story:

```text
You lend Rahim 100,000.
Rahim pays you 10,000 every year.
After 5 years, Rahim returns 100,000.
That is a bond.
```

The safest beginner mindset:

```text
Do not only look at high coupon.
Look at issuer safety, maturity, price, YTM, duration, and risk.
```

Bonds are not magic.

They are structured loans.

Once you understand that, all bond terms become easier.
